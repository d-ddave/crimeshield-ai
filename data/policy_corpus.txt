SECTION 1 — PURPOSE AND SCOPE
-------------------------------
This policy sets out Lloyds Banking Group's obligations and internal controls
for preventing, detecting, and reporting money laundering, terrorist financing,
and other forms of financial crime. It applies to all business lines, including
Retail Banking, Commercial Banking, and Wealth Management.
 
All colleagues are required to remain vigilant for indicators of suspicious
activity and to report concerns promptly through the established Suspicious
Activity Report (SAR) process to the Money Laundering Reporting Officer (MLRO).
 
SECTION 2 — CUSTOMER DUE DILIGENCE (CDD)
------------------------------------------
Standard CDD must be applied to all new customers at onboarding and on a
risk-based schedule thereafter. CDD comprises:
  (a) Identity verification against government-issued documentation
  (b) Verification of residential or registered business address
  (c) Understanding the purpose and intended nature of the business relationship
  (d) Ongoing monitoring of transactions relative to the customer's known profile
 
Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) is mandatory where a customer is identified as:
  - A Politically Exposed Person (PEP) or close associate of a PEP
  - Resident or registered in a FATF grey-listed or black-listed jurisdiction
  - Operating in a high-risk sector (e.g., cash-intensive, correspondent banking,
        money service businesses)
  - Subject to a previous Suspicious Activity Report
 
EDD measures include obtaining senior management approval, establishing the
source of wealth and source of funds, and applying enhanced ongoing monitoring.
 
SECTION 3 — SUSPICIOUS ACTIVITY REPORTING
-------------------------------------------
A SAR must be submitted to the National Crime Agency (NCA) via the SAR Online
system where a colleague knows or suspects, or has reasonable grounds to know
or suspect, that a person is engaged in or attempting money laundering or
terrorist financing.
 
The reporting obligation is triggered by suspicion — not certainty. A colleague
must not conduct the transaction or continue to service the customer while a SAR
is pending consent from the NCA, except where the transaction cannot practicably
be delayed (tipping-off considerations apply).
 
Internal SARs must be submitted to the MLRO within 24 hours of the concern
arising. The MLRO will make the determination on external reporting within 7
working days.
 
Tipping-off: it is a criminal offence under Section 333A of the Proceeds of
Crime Act 2002 to disclose to a customer or third party that a SAR has been
made, or that an investigation is underway or contemplated.
 
SECTION 4 — HIGH-RISK INDICATORS (GENERAL)
--------------------------------------------
The following are general indicators that may, in combination with other factors,
indicate potential money laundering or financial crime:
 
  - Transactions that are inconsistent with the customer's known profile,
        occupation, or stated business purpose
  - Reluctance to provide satisfactory documentation or explanations
  - Multiple accounts at different branches used in a pattern suggesting
        deliberate fragmentation of funds
  - Sudden or unexplained increases in transaction volumes or values
  - Use of third parties to conduct transactions on behalf of a customer
        without a clear legitimate commercial reason
  - Frequent international transfers to or from high-risk jurisdictions
  - Rapid movement of funds through accounts with little or no apparent
        business purpose (layering behaviour)
 
SECTION 5 — ESCALATION AND GOVERNANCE
----------------------------------------
Potential matches against sanctions lists must be escalated to the Financial
Crime Compliance team within 2 hours of identification. All sanctions alerts
must be treated as priority and must not be closed without sign-off from a
Financial Crime Manager or above.
 
Cases involving PEP-flagged customers require senior investigator review before
any case can be moved to a NO_ACTION_TAKEN outcome.

CHAPTER 4 — KNOW YOUR CUSTOMER AND CUSTOMER DUE DILIGENCE
-----------------------------------------------------------
Firms must establish and maintain appropriate risk-based customer due diligence
procedures. The level of due diligence applied should be proportionate to the
money laundering and terrorist financing risk posed by the customer and the
business relationship.
 
Risk factors that increase the level of due diligence required include:
  - Non-face-to-face business relationships
  - Customers from politically unstable countries
  - Customers in sectors associated with higher money laundering risk
  - Complex ownership structures that obscure the beneficial owner
 
Simplified Due Diligence (SDD) may be applied only where the risk of money
laundering or terrorist financing is demonstrably low and the customer is a
regulated financial institution or listed company subject to disclosure
obligations equivalent to those in the UK.
 
CHAPTER 7 — TRADE-BASED MONEY LAUNDERING (TBML)
-------------------------------------------------
Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML) involves the exploitation of international
trade transactions to transfer value across borders and obscure the origins of
criminal proceeds. TBML is one of the most complex and prevalent forms of money
laundering and is frequently used by organised criminal networks.
 
Red flags for Trade-Based Money Laundering include:
 
  (a) Invoice manipulation: over- or under-invoicing of goods or services
          relative to market value. A transaction involving payment significantly
          above or below the market rate for the stated goods, without a plausible
          commercial explanation, is a primary indicator.
 
  (b) Multiple invoicing: the same goods or services are invoiced more than once
          to justify multiple payment transfers. Firms should look for duplicate
          reference numbers, identical amounts paid to different counterparties, or
          sequential invoice numbers from different suppliers for the same delivery.
 
  (c) Falsely described goods or services: the description of goods in shipping
          documents does not match the nature of the declared business, the declared
          country of origin, or the stated destination. Vague descriptions such as
          "general merchandise" or "goods" without further specification are
          indicative.
 
  (d) Payments to or from unrelated third parties: where the party making or
          receiving payment is not the counterparty named in the trade document,
          this may indicate that the trade transaction is being used as a front to
          move funds between unconnected parties.
 
  (e) Use of free trade zones or high-risk jurisdictions: transactions routed
          through free trade zones, particularly those with limited oversight, or
          through countries on the FATF grey or black list without a clear commercial
          rationale, are higher risk.
 
  (f) Unusual payment terms: payment in advance for high-value goods, payment
          via a third-country bank unrelated to either the buyer or seller, or
          payments structured in amounts just below reporting thresholds.
 
  (g) Inconsistency between declared cargo value and shipping costs: where
          insurance premiums or freight costs are disproportionate to the declared
          cargo value, this may indicate manipulation of the stated value.
 
Firms should apply enhanced scrutiny to documentary trade finance transactions,
letters of credit, and open-account trade payments where one or more of these
indicators are present. A combination of three or more indicators in a single
transaction should trigger a SAR consideration.
 
CHAPTER 8 — STRUCTURING AND SMURFING
---------------------------------------
Structuring (also known as smurfing) involves the deliberate breaking up of
large sums of money into smaller amounts to avoid transaction reporting
thresholds. It is a criminal offence under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002
regardless of whether the underlying funds are criminal in origin.
 
Key indicators of structuring activity:
  - Multiple cash deposits of amounts just below the internal or regulatory
        reporting threshold (commonly sub-£1,000 or sub-£10,000 depending on
        the applicable threshold), made in a short period of time across multiple
        branches or accounts
  - Deposits made by multiple individuals at different branches on the same
        or consecutive days, where the individuals are known associates or where
        the funds are subsequently consolidated into a single account
  - Round-number deposits that follow a repetitive pattern in amount and timing
  - Customer exhibits awareness of transaction monitoring thresholds, for
        example by enquiring about the threshold before making a deposit
 
The reporting threshold for cash transactions under the Proceeds of Crime Act
2002 suspicious activity reporting obligation is not a fixed monetary amount.
The obligation is triggered by suspicion, not by a specific value. However,
for internal triage purposes, transactions involving cumulative cash deposits
exceeding £10,000 within a 30-day period from a single customer should be
subject to enhanced review.
 
For structuring alerts specifically, where a customer makes more than 5 cash
deposits each below £2,000 within a 7-day period, this should be escalated
for SAR consideration without further threshold qualification.
 
CHAPTER 9 — AUTHORISED PUSH PAYMENT FRAUD (APP SCAM)
------------------------------------------------------
Authorised Push Payment (APP) fraud involves a victim being deceived into
authorising a payment to an account controlled by a fraudster. APP fraud
represents one of the fastest-growing categories of financial crime in the
United Kingdom and Lloyds Banking Group is required under the PSR Mandatory
Reimbursement Scheme (effective October 2024) to consider reimbursement in
the majority of cases.
 
Indicators of APP scam activity on the receiving account side:
 
  (a) Account is newly opened (within 90 days) and receives one or more large
          inbound transfers immediately followed by rapid onward payments
  (b) Inbound funds are dispersed to multiple accounts within 24–48 hours,
          frequently to crypto exchanges, mule account networks, or international
          beneficiaries
  (c) Account holder has no prior transaction history consistent with receiving
          funds of the magnitude observed
  (d) Multiple victims making payments to the same destination account or sort
          code within a short window (crowdsourced fraud signal)
  (e) Account is associated with false identity documentation or address
          discrepancies identified during onboarding
 
Reporting obligation for APP scam: where a receiving account is identified as
the beneficiary of APP fraud, a SAR must be filed and the account must be
suspended pending investigation. The victim's bank must be notified within
24 hours of identification. Cases involving suspected mule account networks
must be escalated to the Financial Crime Investigations team.
 
CHAPTER 11 — POLITICALLY EXPOSED PERSONS (PEPS)
-------------------------------------------------
A Politically Exposed Person (PEP) is an individual who holds, or has held
within the last 12 months, a prominent public function. This includes:
  - Heads of state and government ministers
  - Senior politicians and members of parliament
  - Senior officials of government departments and judicial bodies
  - Senior executives of state-owned enterprises
  - Senior officials of international organisations
 
PEP status does not automatically indicate criminality. However, due to the
elevated risk of bribery and corruption associated with their position, PEPs
must be subject to Enhanced Due Diligence, including:
  - Senior management approval before establishing or continuing the relationship
  - Establishing the source of wealth (not merely source of funds)
  - Enhanced ongoing monitoring on at least a quarterly basis
 
Family members and known close associates of PEPs must be treated with the
same level of enhanced scrutiny.
 
PEP status must be reviewed at least annually. A customer who ceases to hold
a PEP position transitions to a standard risk profile only after 12 months
have elapsed from the end of their public function, subject to risk assessment.

TYPOLOGY REF NCA-001: INVOICE FRAUD
-------------------------------------
Invoice fraud involves the creation of fraudulent invoices to extract funds
from victim organisations or to justify the movement of criminal proceeds
through a business account.
 
Common patterns:
  - Sole trader or small business accounts receive large payments from dormant
        or recently incorporated companies, followed by rapid dissipation of funds
  - Invoices are issued for services that are vague or unverifiable (e.g.,
        "consultancy," "management fees," "professional services") with no
        supporting contracts or delivery evidence
  - Counterparty companies have no trading history, no web presence, and no
        employees registered at Companies House
  - Multiple invoices to different dormant companies are paid in rapid
        succession (within 24–72 hours), all for similar amounts
  - The receiving account subsequently makes payments to cash-intensive
        businesses, cryptocurrency exchanges, or international transfers
 
SAR filing obligation: where three or more of the above indicators are present
in a single customer's account activity within a 30-day period, a SAR must be
filed within 5 working days of the identification of the pattern.
 
TYPOLOGY REF NCA-002: MONEY MULE ACTIVITY
-------------------------------------------
A money mule is an individual whose account is used — either knowingly or
unknowingly — to receive and transfer criminal proceeds on behalf of a third
party. Mule accounts are a key enabler in the layering stage of money
laundering.
 
Indicators of mule account activity:
  - Account receives multiple inbound transfers from unconnected third parties
        within a short window, with funds rapidly moved onward
  - Account holder's transaction profile is inconsistent with their stated
        occupation or income level
  - Account holder is contacted by the bank and provides explanations that are
        inconsistent, evasive, or coached
  - Address or contact details frequently changed
  - Account is linked to multiple SIM cards or IP addresses in different
        geographic locations
  - Customer was recruited via social media or unsolicited contact promising
        a commission for use of their account
 
SAR filing obligation: mandatory on identification. Account must be suspended
pending investigation.
 
TYPOLOGY REF NCA-003: SANCTIONS EVASION
-----------------------------------------
Sanctions evasion involves the use of intermediaries, shell companies, or
complex transaction layering to obscure connections to sanctioned individuals,
entities, or jurisdictions.
 
Key indicators:
  - Transaction chain involves a jurisdiction on the OFAC, HMT, or UN
        sanctions list, even if the direct counterparty is not sanctioned
  - Payment references are deliberately vague or coded
  - Correspondent bank is located in a jurisdiction known for lax AML controls
  - Beneficial ownership cannot be established due to complex corporate
        structures spanning multiple jurisdictions
  - Customer has changed address or nationality documentation recently,
        potentially to obscure connection to a sanctioned jurisdiction
 
Reporting obligation: all potential sanctions matches must be escalated
immediately. A SAR must be filed in all cases where there is a plausible
connection to a sanctioned entity or jurisdiction, regardless of transaction
value.
 
TYPOLOGY REF NCA-004: CRYPTOCURRENCY-LINKED MONEY LAUNDERING
--------------------------------------------------------------
The use of cryptocurrency as a layering mechanism is a growing trend in
economic crime. While cryptocurrency transactions are visible on public
blockchains, the conversion of criminal cash to crypto — and the subsequent
reconversion to fiat currency via exchanges — provides a degree of obscurity
when combined with mixing services.
 
Indicators:
  - Large cash deposits followed immediately by purchases at cryptocurrency
        exchanges via bank transfer
  - Multiple small transfers to crypto exchanges from the same account,
        potentially structured below monitoring thresholds
  - Inbound funds from cryptocurrency exchange settlement accounts that are
        inconsistent with the customer's stated activity
  - Use of privacy coins or mixing services identified in transaction metadata
 
Reporting obligation: where the value of crypto-linked transactions exceeds
£10,000 in a 30-day period and the source of funds cannot be established,
a SAR must be filed.
 
TYPOLOGY REF NCA-005: PROLIFERATION FINANCING
-----------------------------------------------
Proliferation financing involves the provision of financial services that
support the development or acquisition of weapons capable of mass destruction.
It is subject to the same reporting framework as terrorist financing.
 
Firms must apply enhanced scrutiny to transactions involving:
  - Dual-use goods (goods with both civilian and military applications)
  - Companies in sectors associated with arms manufacturing or military
        procurement, particularly in sanctioned jurisdictions
  - Payments described as being for "technical equipment," "engineering
        components," or "research materials" without clear commercial justification
 
Any suspicion of proliferation financing must be escalated to the MLRO
immediately and a Defence Against Money Laundering (DAML) SAR filed.
